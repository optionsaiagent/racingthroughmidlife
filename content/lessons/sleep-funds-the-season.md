We're not going to tell you how many hours to sleep. We don't know your life. We'll tell you what the full-distance decision did to ours.

## The line we keep coming back to

The full distance isn't a personality. It's a season you pay for in sleep.

We said that when we were deciding whether to go 140.6 and we haven't found a better way to put it. Every long-course season since has been paid for the same way: earlier bedtimes, dropped evenings, a social calendar that got thin for a while. Not forever. For a season.

## What changed after fifty

At thirty a hard week and a bad night could coexist. Now they can't. A long ride on five hours of sleep isn't a long ride. It's a long ride plus two days of feeling wrong, and those two days are where the next workout was supposed to go.

We noticed it most in the mornings. The 5 a.m. alarm is the honest measurement. When it goes off and the body says yes, the week's funded. When it goes off and the body says something else, the smart move is to listen, take the easy day, and keep the season.

## What we actually did

- **Bedtime first.** The workout's at dawn because of the heat, so the bedtime's early because of the workout. We quit negotiating the bedtime. It's the one line item that pays for everything else.
- **Easy weeks in ink.** The easy week goes on the calendar when the block gets planned, not when we feel like we need it. By the time we feel like we need it, we've needed it for a week.
- **Real recovery after the big days.** The week after a marathon or a long-course race is a rest week. Not a light week. Rest. Walk, swim easy, sleep. The [week after the marathon](/notes/the-week-after-the-marathon) note has the details.

## Seasons end

One more thing about paying for a season in sleep: seasons end. That's not failing. The full distance isn't who we are. It's a thing we do for a few months, and then we go back to being two people who race the local calendar and sleep like normal humans. Knowing there's an end makes the middle survivable.
