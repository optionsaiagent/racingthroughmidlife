A bike fit is an hour or two with somebody who knows what they're doing, adjusting the bike to your body instead of the other way around. Saddle height, reach, cleat position, the angle of things you didn't know had angles.

I had mine the third week of August 2023, in the middle of a season with a 112-mile ride and the Marathon Readiness Series in it. It should have happened a year earlier. Why did I wait? I don't have a good answer, and I've looked for one. I'm going to say the year-earlier part a couple more times before this is over, so you know it's the point.

## Why it matters more now

A body in its forties or fifties has opinions. A back that put up with a bad position at thirty doesn't anymore, and a knee tracking slightly wrong on every pedal stroke will let you know about it somewhere around mile forty of a long ride, and then it'll keep letting you know for the rest of the week.

For me the fit wasn't about speed. It was about being able to ride long without paying for it on the run, or in the next week's training, or in a rib that was already unhappy. Mine was.

## What changed

I'm not going to list saddle millimeters. What I can say is the long rides after the fit were different rides. Less shifting around on the saddle. Less of the hand numbness I'd been treating as normal (it's not normal, and I'd been telling myself it was). A position I could hold for hours, which is the entire job of a position when the ride's 100 miles.

## The order I'd do it in now

Fit first, then the bike. That's the order I'd do it in now, as a fit on an old bike beats no fit on a new one. Jay's [tri bike showed up in July](/lessons/the-bike-came-after-the-race), and the honest version of that story is that both of us should have paid for a fit before either of us paid for a frame. A year earlier. There, that's twice.

If I could do one bike thing over, it'd be this one. The person who adjusts the bike to me, a year earlier. That's three.
