We live here, and we still get it wrong. That should tell you something about how it goes for visitors.

## Why humidity is the whole story

Heat by itself is a number on a forecast. Humidity decides whether your body can do anything about it. Sweat cools you by evaporating, and when the air is already full of water it doesn't evaporate, it just runs down your arms and into your shoes. You're losing the fluid and getting none of the cooling for it.

What that means in practice is that an effort that felt easy in dry air feels hard here at the same pace, and your heart rate climbs through a long session even when nothing else changes. That drift isn't a fitness problem, it's physics, and pretending otherwise is how people cook themselves at mile nine of a half that started at 5:30 in the morning. The number we look at is the dew point, not the temperature. When the dew point's in the 70s, which is most of the summer here, every session is a heat session whether the plan calls it one or not.

## Dawn is a temperature, not a mood

Every long workout we do starts in the dark, and not because we're especially disciplined. It's the only hour the work can happen. By 8 a.m. the pavement's radiating, by 10 the shade's gone, and a long run that starts at 7 finishes in a completely different race than the one it started in.

The local races know this. The Honolulu Marathon starts at 5 a.m. Hibiscus starts early. Honu in June starts with a swim at first light and then sends you into the lava fields for a bike ride where shade is a rumor. So the alarm's early, and everything else in the week follows from that.

## Water and salt, not a product

We don't sell electrolytes and we're not going to recommend a brand. What we can tell you is what we usually do: drink before the session, carry more water than seems reasonable, and put salt on real food in the days around a long effort. In this humidity the sweat rate is high and the sweat is salty, and a long day without replacing both of those ends badly.

On a hot long ride we stop for water more often than the plan says. On a long run we carry it. On race day we hit every aid station from the first one, whether we feel like it or not, because by the time you feel like you need it you're already behind.

## What a mainland plan gets wrong here

Any plan written somewhere cool assumes your body can cool itself. Here it can't, so a few things follow: paces are slower for the same effort, which is correct and not a failure, long sessions have to start earlier and end sooner than the plan thinks, recovery takes longer because the body worked harder than the pace suggests, and every race is a heat race, including the ones in what the calendar calls winter. We don't adjust a plan with a formula. We adjust it with the alarm clock and the dew point.

## Honu, if you want proof

My first 70.3, in June 2023, is this whole note in one race. Swim at first light in the ocean, then 56 miles through black lava with the wind coming off the water and not a tree for miles, then a half marathon in the heat of the day. The people who finished well were the ones who respected the heat from the first pedal stroke. The ones who didn't were walking by mile four of the run.
