## Who this is for

You are somewhere between 45 and 65. You used to be active, or you never were, and you have started to notice that the body bills interest now. Stairs are a data point. You have seen the people in the finisher photos and thought, quietly, that some of them look like you.

You do not want a coach. You do not want a plan with twelve tabs. You want to know what two ordinary people actually did, in what order, and what it cost.

That is this page. We are not trainers. We are not doctors. We are two Army veterans in Honolulu who started late and kept going. Everything here is what we did, not what you should do. Talk to your own doctor first, then read on.

## What we did first, and would do again

We did not start with a triathlon. We started with a run we could already finish.

For years the longest thing either of us ran was the Great Aloha Run, 8.15 miles from Aloha Tower to the stadium on Presidents' Day. It has a huge field, a real start-line feeling, and a distance a beginner can finish upright. We did that, and not much else, for a long time. That was fine. Staying on one rung is not failure. It is a rung.

The thing that changed was not motivation. It was a date. In December 2021 Michelle watched the Honolulu Marathon and said we should run it the next year for Jay's fiftieth. Jay had never wanted a marathon. He did not like running. But now there was a date, and a person he did not want to let down, and twelve months.

So we built the next rung: a half marathon, Hibiscus, in May 2022. Then the marathon that December. Then, because the marathon made a long day feel survivable, an Olympic-distance triathlon we had done badly in 2014. Then a 70.3 on a hard course. Then a 112-mile bike ride around the island. Then the full distance.

Nobody skipped a rung. Every one of them was in the neighborhood of something we had already suffered through.

## The three things that actually mattered

**Dawn, most days.** Not a heroic block of training. A boring one. In Honolulu the only temperature that lets the work happen is the one before sunrise, so the alarm is early and the workout is whatever fits before the day starts. Consistency at 5 a.m. beat every ambitious Saturday we ever planned. If you live somewhere cooler, your version might be lunch or evening. The rule is the same: pick the hour you will actually keep.

**The next rung, not the far one.** A 10K person who signs up for an Ironman because a podcast said midlife is a starting line has skipped five rungs. We picked the next distance that scared us a little and was still close to what we had done. A half before the marathon. An Olympic before the 70.3. The 112-mile ride before the 140.6. It took longer. It also worked.

**Whose day is it.** We train as a couple, and our engines do not match. Jay is faster. Michelle's PRs are the story. The only couples rule we would defend in public is this: say out loud whose day it is, and the other person sits on that pace even when it feels easy, especially when it feels easy. Read [the whole rule](/lessons/whose-day-is-it). It has saved more weekends than any workout.

## What we would tell you at dinner

- Sign up for the race after the long run exists, not before. The internet says commit first so you cannot back out. Sometimes that puts a person who has never run 13 miles into a 26.2 they will remember as a medical event.
- Race in the shoe you trained in. Put the race shoe on a long run before the race. Do not trust a drop your calf has not met.
- Eat what you have already suffered with. Race morning is not the day for a new bar.
- Your first triathlon should be a sprint or an Olympic, close to home, where a bad transition ruins an hour and not a trip.
- The bike can wait. Jay did his first 70.3 on the bike he had. The triathlon bike showed up a month later, after the race proved he would keep doing this.
- Write the plan in pencil. A rib, a work trip, a week of family visiting, all of those happened to us in one year. The plan bent. The season did not break.
- Fund the season with sleep. At fifty, recovery is not a luxury line item. It is the budget.

## The body that bills interest

We are not going to tell you what your heart rate should be or how much protein to eat. We do not know your labs.

What we will say is that things took longer than they did at thirty. A hard week needed an easy week behind it. A rib injury that would have been a nuisance at 25 took a real chunk out of a spring. Long runs left us tired for a day, not an hour.

None of that stopped anything. It just set the pace of the year. If you plan for a slower climb, you get to keep climbing.

## If you live somewhere hot

Then you have a fourth sport, and it does not care about your fitness numbers. Humidity changes how much you drink, when you train, and what your pace means. Everything we learned about that is in [Heat is the fourth sport](/notes/heat-is-the-fourth-sport).
