About 18.6 miles, seven weeks before the marathon. Of the five races in the series, this is the one that matters most and the one that's easiest to get wrong, and I understand exactly why people get it wrong, as I wanted to get it wrong myself.

## The trap

A 30K is long enough to feel like an accomplishment and short enough to race. Run it at real effort and it's a marathon in disguise, with a marathon's worth of recovery attached, and there wasn't a marathon's worth of recovery available because there was a marathon in seven weeks. I did that math more than once, because the number on the card is right there tempting you.

So we ran it at marathon effort. The [rule for the series](/lessons/let-the-calendar-be-the-plan) is that from the 25K on, every race is a rehearsal, and the 30K is the one in full costume.

## What I was checking

Breakfast: the one I'd eat in December. Shoes: the pair that would get the marathon bib. Fueling: what was in my pockets and whether it stayed down after mile twelve in the heat. Pace: could the first ten miles be boring on purpose, and what did the last five feel like once they had been. Boring on purpose is my specialty (I've been practicing it my whole life).

That's what a 30K is for, and a time isn't. Mine was 3:27:55, 11:09 a mile, and it's here because it's on the card and not because it means anything. Jay ran 2:42:08 and was eighth in his age group, and I was fifteenth in mine.

## What I took from it

If I took one thing from the whole readiness series, it was this race. I could have raced the 30K and had a great October and a bad December, or I could rehearse it and let December be a longer version of a day I'd already done. I'd decided the people who look strong at mile 22 of the Honolulu Marathon are the ones who were bored at mile 5 of this one, and I wanted to be one of them. Seven weeks later I'd find out.
