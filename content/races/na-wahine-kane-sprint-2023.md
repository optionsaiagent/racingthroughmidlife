Six weeks after a 70.3, a sprint triathlon is a strange thing to sign up for. It was also the smartest race I did that summer, and I say that knowing how it sounds next to Honu.

## Why a sprint

There were two reasons. The [triathlon bike had arrived the second week of July](/lessons/the-bike-came-after-the-race), and a bike that has never seen a transition area should see a small one first. And after the long, slow, hot build to Honu, a short hard race is a reset: a fast swim, a fast bike, a fast run, and home before the heat gets serious.

A sprint is also where you actually learn transitions. At a 70.3 a bad transition costs a few minutes out of a day that's hours long, so nobody practices them, us included. At a sprint a bad transition is a real chunk of the race, so you learn to lay out the towel, rack the bike the right way, and get the shoes on without sitting down. The numbers from this one say it worked: 1:30 in the first transition and 1:23 in the second, on a bike I'd owned for a couple of weeks.

## The course

It's a local sprint on Oahu with a swim, a short bike, and a short run. I raced the Kane division and came in third in my age group, 1:16:24 on the clock: 13:35 in the water, 36:29 on the bike, 23:27 for the run, and the two transitions in between. I'm not going to describe the course in detail, because I'd be reconstructing it from the video and the video does that better, so watch it.

## Make the first one a sprint

If you've never done a triathlon, make the first one a sprint, not because you couldn't do an Olympic but because a sprint teaches you everything about the sport that isn't fitness: the swim start, the transition, and the first mile of running off the bike. Learn that stuff where a mistake costs minutes instead of the whole day, and then go longer.

And if you buy a bike mid-season, race it at a sprint before it goes anywhere that counts. Four weeks after this one, the same bike did [112 miles around the island](/races/dick-evans-112-2023).
